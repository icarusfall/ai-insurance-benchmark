A toy catastrophe model

Could insurance carry the tail of frontier AI?

The AI Underwriting Company has proposed that frontier labs insure each other through a mutual: pooled capital, shared safety standards, audits with teeth. They sketch the structure; this page puts rough numbers on it. It starts from their mutual, and then asks whether the labs' own captives, a commercial insurer or a government backstop would carry the same losses better (who carries it). Move the levers and watch the premium, the capital a regulator would ask for, and how much of the harm would actually be paid.

This is a toy model to take the idea out for a walk, not to definitively price catastrophe. Every number is a starting point with its source shown; many are judgement calls labelled as such. The interesting output is which levers matter, not any one figure.

The model

Pull the levers

Three perils: an engineered epidemic a frontier model helped cause, a cyber catastrophe it enabled, and a lab's own agents running amok, as OpenAI's did at Hugging Face in July 2026. Each has a rate, a fat-tailed severity, and the share of the harm a court would pin on the lab. The mutual covers each member up to a limit per event and per year, backed by premium, paid-in capital and calls on members. 100,000 simulated years per setting.

How often

Three ways to count

The AIUC authors draw a line between two ways of pricing a catastrophe. An actuary works from the record: how often has this happened, and how bad was it? A catastrophe modeller maps the chain of events that leads to harm and puts a probability on each link. For AI the record is a handful of incidents, so the first method has little to work with. The second forces you to say what you believe about each step, which is useful in itself for deciding where safety work matters. The model can count either way, or use its default: rates set from expert forecasts and parallels with other industries. Severity, attribution and everything downstream stay the same; only how often events happen changes. The choice here drives the whole page.

Sensitivity

Which levers move the answer

Each bar swings one parameter from the low to the high end of its range, with everything else at the central settings. The cover and capital levers have no range, so they are halved and doubled. Every run uses the same simulated years, so the bars show each parameter's effect, not noise. The answer hangs most on how much cyber harm a court would pin on a lab (the next chapter builds that up from case law), then on members' revenue and on how often AI cyber catastrophes happen. Bio dominates the harm in the world but hardly moves the premium: limits and near-zero attribution keep it off the mutual's books.

Who pays

How much of a cyber catastrophe a court would pin on a lab

When an attacker uses a frontier model to take down a payment system, the attacker is the obvious wrongdoer, but the attacker will never pay: it is a state, or a gang beyond reach. What the mutual owes depends on how much a court would put on the lab instead. That is a chain of three legal questions, each with case law behind it.

  1. Does a claim survive? A criminal act usually breaks the chain of causation. It does not where stopping that act was the point of the safeguards the defendant neglected (Restatement § 449). Pennsylvania's Supreme Court applied this to a data breach in Dittman.
  2. How much of the loss can be claimed at all? Most of a cyber catastrophe's cost is business interruption at companies with no tie to the lab. Under the economic loss rule, courts across the country refuse negligence claims for purely economic loss after a man-made disaster. California's Supreme Court did so unanimously after the Aliso Canyon gas leak (Gas Leak Cases), though it left open whether the rule holds "in cyberspace". What remains is damage to systems and data, injuries, and customers, whose contracts may cap what they get.
  3. Of that, how much does the lab pay? Where liability is joint and several, the lab pays the attacker's share as well as its own: that is the rule in Restatement § 14 and for economic damages in California. Where it is several only, as in Idaho, the lab pays just its own share of the fault, and a jury will put most of the fault on the attacker (§ 14).

Multiplied together, the central reading puts about 7% of an AI-enabled cyber catastrophe on the lab. Move the links below and the cyber attribution on the levers board moves with them.

Capability over time

If the models keep getting better

The rates above describe today. How fast they grow depends on how fast AI gets more capable. Capability here is METR's time horizon: the length of software task, timed by how long it takes a human, that an AI agent can finish half the time. The best model METR has measured, in April 2026, manages about 17 hours. Four paths from there: AI 2027's speeding-up trend at the pace its authors say reality is running; METR's fitted trend since 2023; its slower trend since 2019; and a plateau that flattens towards a ceiling you choose. METR's data show no flattening yet, so the plateau is an assumption, not a finding.

Each peril's rate rises with the horizon at its own elasticity, up to a cap. Bio barely moves, because most of the uplift has already happened. A lab's own agents scale roughly with how long they can run unsupervised. Members' revenue grows too, so the premium can shrink as a share of revenue even while claims rise. The projection stops when the horizon reaches the stop level. Past that, the AI 2027 authors expect AGI within about a year, and loss of control is out of scope, so the model has nothing useful to say. How many years come before the stop is itself the result.

Uses every setting from the levers above, re-simulated each year (20,000 years each). A plateau is not necessarily good news for the mutual: if capability saturates, it spreads to small open models (Fogelson et al.), misuse moves to non-members' models, and more of the harm goes uninsured.

The market lens

What the prediction markets say

Prediction markets price some of the same events. Few questions match the model's perils exactly, so each one below says how we read it. A question about an incident before some year covers a window in which, on the central capability path, risk is growing. We take that growth out before comparing with today's rate, since the Over time chapter adds it back. Read that way, the markets agree with the model's central rates more than they disagree. The Markets preset, beside Optimistic and Pessimistic above, uses their numbers: the agent and cyber rates, the stop horizon set where the central path is at the markets' median date for AGI, and member calls trimmed by the bankruptcy risk the markets price.

Capital in two languages

What a regulator would ask for

The same simulated losses, read the American way and the European way. The biggest difference for a mutual: under Solvency II, members' promises to pay more later can count as capital (up to half the requirement); under US statutory accounting, they don't.

The US formula has a blind spot. NAIC risk-based capital charges reserves and premium by line of business at industry factors, and charges catastrophe only for earthquake and hurricane. As written, it never sees the tail of an AI catastrophe, so the mutual's ratio is in the tens of thousands of percent. The second ratio adds the mutual's 1-in-100 year loss as if regulators charged it like a hurricane. A domicile would more likely ask for that through its own review than through the formula.

Simplified on purpose: one catastrophe-and-premium risk module and a run-off risk margin for Solvency II; for the US, reserve, premium and operational risk without asset or credit risk. The Solvency II and NAIC factors are checked against the rules (see the model's own constants). See the model's notes in catmodel/capital_s2.py and capital_us.py.

Who carries it

A mutual is one option, not the only one

Most of what is written about insuring frontier AI starts from AIUC's mutual. But nothing in the losses above depends on who holds them. The same simulated years can be carried by the labs together, by each lab alone, by a commercial insurer or reinsurer, or in layers. The structure decides three things: what capital costs, whether pooling cuts how much is needed, and who has a reason to watch the labs.

Pooling matters more than the mutual form. A lab insuring itself through its own captive pays about four times what it pays in a pool, because it must hold capital for its own bad year alone. A diversified insurer is cheapest, because an AI catastrophe does not coincide with a hurricane, so it asks less for its capital. That flatters it, though: the same event would hit its cyber and technology books, which is the correlation Leung et al. single out as AI's genuinely new insurance problem. Members' callable capital lifts the Solvency II ratio but does not change the premium.

What each structure is good for

An insurer's case as a check on the labs rests on independence and on having its own money at stake. A mutual scores worst on independence; its case is different: members pay for each other's losses, so they have reason to police each other, as the mill owners of Factory Mutual did from 1835. Both are fair arguments, but they are different ones. A plausible answer is not one carrier but a stack: the labs pool the frequent losses, reinsurers price the layer above from outside, and government takes the far tail.

The qualitative columns are judgement. Today's standalone AI liability cover runs to about $25m per insured (Armilla), against AIUC's $1bn minimum per member.

Capital markets

A cat bond on the mutual

The same distribution, sold to investors: a bond that loses principal when the mutual's annual claims pass the attachment, and is wiped out at exhaustion. The first cyber cat bonds priced at about ten times their expected loss, against about three times for natural catastrophe: investors charge heavily for a peril nobody can model. An AI cat bond would sit at least as far out. And unlike hurricanes, an AI catastrophe is plausibly correlated with the rest of an investor's portfolio.

Spreads three ways: a multiple of expected loss (the lever above), Lane's (2000) power law, and a Wang transform. Both of the last two are re-fitted to 58 natural catastrophe and 9 cyber bonds from 2023–26 (see the re-fit). Cyber bonds price at about twice the natural catastrophe curve; we price an AI bond like cyber, which is a judgement and arguably generous.

The hard case

Is AI-assisted bio risk insurable?

Usually asserted, rarely shown. Here are nine tests, run on the bio peril alone with the central parameters. The verdict: bio risk can be held as a small, incentive-bearing slice (which is what the mutual proposal asks for) but cannot be insured as compensation.

  • No finite mean. Both pandemic tail fits we found put the tail index below 1, so expected harm grows with wherever you cap it.
  • A capped slice is cheap. $1bn per lab costs about $24m a year per lab, a quarter of a percent of a $10bn revenue.
  • It compensates almost nobody. About 2% of what courts would pin on labs; covering half would need about $75bn per lab per event.
  • Limits turn a tail problem into a frequency problem. Once limits bind, the tail hardly matters; the price hangs on how often AI helps cause a catastrophe.
  • Law decides who carries it. Strict liability multiplies labs' exposure tenfold without changing what the mutual pays.
  • Solvency II's 1-in-200 standard sits on a 1-in-200 peril, so the capital charge flips between nothing and the whole limit.
Sensitivity: tail index × attribution × limit

Provenance

Where the numbers come from

Every parameter traces to an entry here, from one of four viewpoints: built up from first principles, a parallel from another fat-tailed industry, a market price or forecast, or an expert elicitation. Each says how far it has been checked.

The model's own constants

Besides the levers, the model hard-codes a few numbers taken from regulation and the pricing literature. Each was checked against the text it came from. Two needed correcting: Lane's pricing formula had been fed percentages where his paper uses decimals, which overstated its spreads by about a third, and the Solvency II own-funds rule carried a cap that the regulation doesn't have. The two cat bond pricing constants have since been re-fitted to 2023–26 bonds.

About

What this is, and isn't

A personal project, vibe-coded with Claude Code, responding to the AI Underwriting Company's proposal for a frontier AI mutual (Trout, Kvist and Dattani, Lawfare, September 2026). The name nods to MAIM, Mutual Assured AI Malfunction, from Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang's Superintelligence Strategy.

It is not a price, a recommendation, or a view on any real company. Loss of control is out of scope by construction: nobody would be left to pay or receive a claim. Parameters marked as judgement are there to be argued with; the model's Python source and the parameter registry sit beside an earlier project, an LLM benchmark in which models run an AI-liability insurer.
