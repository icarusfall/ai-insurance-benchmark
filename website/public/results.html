Who kept the shareholders happy

Each model ran the same insurer, against the same four scripted rivals, on the same simulated decades. Below is how its shareholders' wealth evolved, quarter by quarter, measured against what they required: 2% a quarter.

These games were played under environment . The explainer describes the current rules (), which add a graded capital ladder and regulated dividends; results are only compared within one version, and the current rules will be re-run.

How to read it. Lines start at 1.0. A line that drifts down slowly is holding capital that earns less than shareholders want. A line that drops sharply is an insolvency in at least half the seeds: equity goes to zero and recent dividends are clawed back. The reference actuary was built with a near-correct model of the hidden world; the language models start with none.

Summary

The numbers

Models are ranked by "share of the reference's R": each game's R divided by the reference actuary's R on the same world, median across games. This is fairer than median R alone, because models played different numbers of seeds and some worlds are kinder than others. "Beat the reference" counts seeds where the model's final R exceeded the reference actuary's on the same world. Cost is the API bill per game; minutes are wall-clock time per 40-quarter game.

Nerve

Same average, different nerve

The ranking uses medians, which reward consistency. Averages tell a different story. Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol return almost exactly the same R on average, about 1.7. Opus's results, though, are spread more than three times as widely: its best game is the best in the benchmark, and in nine of twenty it failed to earn the shareholders their 2% a quarter. An investor who owned all twenty of either company would have done about equally well. An investor who owned one would much rather it were Sol.

DeepSeek V4 Flash has the highest average of any model, helped by a few very good games, which its median plays down. Sonnet 5.5 averages R 1.0, just meeting the hurdle, despite being seized nine times: its good games pay for its bad ones.

The bill

What it cost, and why

The models' bills for one game run from about 12 cents to $4.50, nearly forty-fold. The best score and the biggest bill don't go together. DeepSeek V4 Flash gets within three points of GPT-6.1 Sol for about a seventh of the price. Opus is the most expensive model and comes third.

A bill is the price per token times the tokens used, and models differ wildly in how many tokens they use. Each quarter is a fresh conversation. Every call re-sends the whole conversation so far (the briefing, the tool results and the model's own replies). The model is charged for reading that again, at a tenth of the price if the provider has cached it. On top of that it pays for what it writes, including hidden reasoning, at two to six times the input price.

The token counts are measured; the explanations in the cards below are our best guesses. Costs come from the call logs. OpenRouter (DeepSeek) reports what it billed. For the other providers, the reported token counts are multiplied by list prices, so treat those figures as close estimates.

Pays to remember

Claude Opus 5.5

Over half of Opus's bill (56%) goes on writing its conversation into Anthropic's prompt cache. Anthropic charges a quarter extra to write to the cache and a tenth of the price to read from it, so the cache only pays off when it is read several times. Opus does a quarter's work in two or three large calls, firing several tools at once, then the conversation is thrown away. Sonnet and Haiku have the same habit. A harness that kept one conversation per game would probably cut this, at the price of ever-longer prompts.

Looks before it leaps

GPT-6.1 Sol

Sol makes about six calls a quarter, two and a half times as many as Opus, and reads 3.8 million tokens a game. Most of that comes from cache at a tenth of the price, but there is so much of it that fresh input is still two fifths of the bill. Our guess is that it checks its book, the market and the applicants before each decision. It is also the best of the three models that never went insolvent, so the habit may be worth paying for.

Thinks out loud, cheaply

DeepSeek V4 and V4.1 Flash

V4.1 Flash writes about 880,000 tokens a game, ten times as much as Opus, nearly all of it reasoning. V4 Flash makes eleven calls a quarter and reads eleven million tokens a game. Both stay cheap only because their tokens cost a twentieth to a hundredth of the frontier models' price. They pay in time instead: about two hours a game for V4 Flash. They were also the only models that kept repeating the same tool call until the harness's loop breaker stopped them.

Barely thinks

Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite

About 54 output tokens per call: it acts with almost no visible reasoning. Most of its bill is reading. It finishes a game in four minutes for 30 cents and lands mid-table, ahead of models that cost seven times as much.

The price list isn't the bill

Sonnet against Sol

Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol charge the same per token, yet Sonnet's games cost a third less because it makes fewer than two calls a quarter. Opus's tokens cost twice Sol's, but its bill is only a third higher. The pilots taught the same lesson more painfully: Gemini 3.5 Flash took 477 calls to finish one game and cost $7.29.

Pilots

Models we only tried once

Before committing seeds, every new model played one pilot game to measure what a full game really costs. Most stopped there, usually for reasons list prices would not have predicted. One game says little about rank, so none of these are in the chart or the table above.

Too expensive

Gemini 3.5 Flash, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Grok 4.7

Gemini 3.5 Flash played well but took 477 model calls to finish one game (Opus took 104), each step re-reading the whole conversation, so a game cost $7.29. Gemini 3.8 Flash did the same for $4.71 and a weak score. Grok 4.7 cost $8.29 and took two and a half hours. Cheap per token is not cheap per game.

Ran out of words

MiniMax M3.1 Flash, GLM 5.3 FlashX

Both kept hitting the 16,000-token limit on a single reply, MiniMax 33 times, often mid-thought and before acting. MiniMax left 15 quarters unfinished. Both went insolvent. The limit is the same for every model and was kept, so results stay comparable.

Too slow

Kimi K2.6

At its default setting it reasoned until it ran out of output allowance, twice, without ever acting. With reasoning effort set to medium (the one exception to identical settings for every model) it scored R 1.74, but one game took 3 hours 13 minutes and cost $4.95.

Did not finish

Mistral Large 4, Qwen 3.8 Max

Mistral was stopped early when its cost per game was heading past $6. Qwen's first attempt crashed our code (a reply with reasoning but no text, which we now handle). Its second was heading for $13 a game when the connection dropped at quarter 14.

Promising

GPT-6.1 Terra

The best pilot: R 2.51 for $1.18. Its sibling GPT-6.1 Sol was taken to twenty games instead. Terra may join it in a later wave.

All pilots were played on seed 0, a generous world on which the reference scored 3.62. A pilot that went on to the full run counts as that model's seed 0.

Caveats

Read this before quoting anything

  • Small samples. A handful of seeds per model. Outcomes vary a great deal between seeds and between runs of the same model on the same seed. Treat this as a first look, not a ranking.
  • Few games per model. Twenty seeds per model. That is enough to separate the top two from the rest, but neighbours a few points apart are still not reliably ordered. At ten seeds Opus, Gemini 4 Argon and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash looked level with the leaders; the next ten worlds were less kind to their bolder strategies. A model's own randomness also varies from run to run.
  • Not every model was run in full. Several played one pilot game only (above). GPT-6 Luna, DeepSeek V4 and V4.1 Flash, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5 are the inexpensive tier.
  • Three waves, one set of rules. Wave 5 added newer models nine days after wave 4, on the same ten worlds (seeds 0 to 9) and the same rules. Wave 6 played every model on ten more worlds (seeds 10 to 19), with the wave 5 code. The only change in between ends a model's quarter once it repeats the identical tool call five times in a row. It fired only for DeepSeek: in seven of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash's games and in one of DeepSeek V4 Flash's (seed 8, re-run in wave 5). DeepSeek V4 Flash's other games, played without it, are not quite like for like.
  • Unequal information. The reference and rivals have a near-correct risk model; the language models do not. Beating the reference is hard by design.
  • One environment version. Results are only comparable within one version; these were played under .
